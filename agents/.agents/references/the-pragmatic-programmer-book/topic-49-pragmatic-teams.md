## Topic 49: Pragmatic Teams

Pragmatic teams multiply individual developer strengths by applying pragmatic software engineering principles collectively. Small, stable groups of cross-functional team members build end-to-end software efficiently through shared responsibility, continuous improvement, and automated processes.

### The Pragmatic Approach

- **Maintain Small, Stable Teams**: Keep team size under 10 to 12 members. Small teams minimize communication overhead, foster trust, and maintain strong mutual reliance.
- **Own Quality Collectively**: Make product quality the shared responsibility of every team member. Fix minor defects promptly to preserve high standards across the project.
- **Monitor Environmental Changes**: Track scope expansion, schedule shifts, and feature requests continuously using visual metrics such as burnup charts.
- **Schedule Skill and Process Improvements**: Allocate explicit time on the team schedule for maintenance, process refinement, technology evaluation, and team training.
- **Communicate as a Unified Entity**: Establish a distinct team identity and present consistent external communication, while encouraging active internal debate.
- **Enable Frictionless Communication**: Share progress, insights, and questions immediately through direct channels to prevent duplicated effort.
- **Build End-to-End Functionality**: Form cross-functional teams with all required skills, including frontend, backend, database, and quality assurance, to deliver complete features iteratively.
- **Automate Repeated Tasks**: Automate code formatting, test suites, and deployment pipelines using custom tools and continuous integration workflows.
- **Provide Light Structure**: Establish minimal structural boundaries to support developers, then avoid over-engineering or adding unnecessary features.

### Common Mistakes

- **Growing Teams Too Large**: Expanding team size beyond 10 to 12 members, which increases communication paths exponentially and degrades collaboration.
- **Delegating Quality to an External Officer**: Assigning quality control to a single role instead of embedding quality into every developer's daily contributions.
- **Ignoring Incremental Scope Creep**: Overlooking gradual increases in requirements, feature additions, or environment changes until the team misses project goals.
- **Leaving Learning to Free Moments**: Reserving time for system maintenance, process retrospectives, or skill development only when free time occurs.
- **Working in Silos**: Delaying team updates or questions until formal periodic meetings, which causes duplicate work and isolated code.
- **Creating Hand-off Gates Between Roles**: Separating functional roles into distinct phases, which creates approval bottlenecks and delays release flow.
- **Executing Build and Deploy Steps Manually**: Performing manual testing, code formatting, or server deployments instead of building automated tooling.
