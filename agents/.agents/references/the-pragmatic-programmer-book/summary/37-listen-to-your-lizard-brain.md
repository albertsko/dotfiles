## 37. Listen to Your Lizard Brain

Instincts are wordless responses to innate or learned patterns in the nonconscious brain. Programming experience builds tacit knowledge of effective techniques, warning signs, and likely causes that surfaces as nervousness, reluctance, or unusual effort. Such discomfort may reflect fear of mistakes, doubts about your competence, or work that feels beyond you, but it may also signal a flawed structure, an unsuitable design, the wrong problem, or mounting bugs, so forcing yourself onward can suppress useful feedback. Pause to let the concern become clear, externalize it when needed, and use disposable prototypes to make exploration safe; apply the same attention to unfamiliar code, designs, and requirements so you can recognize patterns and address hidden problems early.

### The Pragmatic Approach

- Notice unease, reluctance, and unexpected effort, and treat them as signals worth investigating.
- Stop working when the code pushes back, step away from the keyboard, and let your thoughts settle through a walk, a meal, a conversation, or sleep.
- Externalize a vague concern by drawing the code or explaining the problem to another person, preferably a nonprogrammer, or a rubber duck.
- Reframe a blank page as a disposable prototype that may fail and must be discarded even if it succeeds; for resistant existing code, stash the current work and prototype something similar.
- Put a visible “I’m prototyping” reminder beside the screen, and consult it when doubts return.
- State in one sentence what the prototype should teach you, then explore one uncertain framework behavior, algorithm, interaction, or alternative implementation.
- Address any concern that becomes concrete; if unease remains after the experiment, step away and externalize the problem again.
- Delete the prototype once it restores understanding and momentum, then write the production code from what you learned.
- Time-box a scary or difficult task to an hour or two, commit to deleting the result, and identify what the experiment taught you.
- Record unfamiliar choices in existing code without assuming they are worse, identify recurring patterns, and infer the reasoning behind them.
- In a supportive environment, voice and examine discomfort about designs or requirements before hidden problems become failures.
