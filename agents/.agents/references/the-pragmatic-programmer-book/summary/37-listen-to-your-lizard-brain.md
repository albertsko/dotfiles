## 37. Listen to Your Lizard Brain

Programming experience builds tacit knowledge of effective techniques, warning signs, and likely causes that surfaces without words as nervousness, reluctance, or unusual effort. Such discomfort may reflect fear of mistakes, but it may also signal a flawed structure, an unsuitable design, the wrong problem, or mounting bugs, so forcing yourself onward can suppress useful feedback. Pause to let the concern become clear, externalize it when needed, and use disposable prototypes to make exploration safe; apply the same attention to unfamiliar code, designs, and requirements so you can recognize patterns and address hidden problems early.

### The Pragmatic Approach

- Notice unease, reluctance, and unexpected effort, and treat them as signals worth investigating.
- Stop working when the code pushes back, step away from the keyboard, and let your thoughts settle through a walk, a meal, a conversation, or sleep.
- Externalize a vague concern by drawing the code or explaining the problem to another person or a rubber duck.
- Reframe a blank page or resistant implementation as a disposable prototype that may fail and must be discarded even if it succeeds.
- State in one sentence what the prototype should teach you, then explore one uncertain framework behavior, algorithm, interaction, or alternative implementation.
- Address any concern that becomes concrete; if unease remains after the experiment, step away and externalize the problem again.
- Delete the prototype once it restores understanding and momentum, then write the production code from what you learned.
- Record unfamiliar choices in existing code, identify recurring patterns, and infer the reasoning behind them.
- Voice and examine discomfort about designs or requirements before hidden problems become failures.
