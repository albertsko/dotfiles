## 15. Estimating

Estimation turns incomplete questions into useful, explicitly approximate answers that prevent surprises and build an intuitive sense of feasibility and optimization priorities. A sound estimate establishes the needed accuracy, scope, and assumptions, draws on comparable experience, and uses the simplest model that adequately represents the system. Breaking the model into interacting components exposes its parameters; defensible values matter most for parameters that multiply or divide because they usually drive the result, so vary those drivers to produce a range of scenarios and treat strange results as evidence of faulty arithmetic, assumptions, or modeling. Express the result in units that convey only the accuracy the estimate can support, then compare estimates with outcomes to improve future judgment. For complex projects, evidence from thin, complete slices of functionality supports progressively more reliable schedules than a fixed prediction made before the team, technology, risks, environment, and delivery rate are understood.

### The Pragmatic Approach

- Ask what decision the estimate will support and how much accuracy it requires.
- Define the scope and state the assumptions that bound the answer.
- Quote durations of 1–15 days in days, 3–6 weeks in weeks, and 8–20 weeks in months; think hard before estimating work beyond 20 weeks.
- Consult people who have solved comparable problems before building a model.
- Build the simplest useful model, split it into interacting components, identify each component's parameters, and stop refining when more modeling effort adds little accuracy.
- Reconsider the original request when modeling reveals a substantially cheaper variant, and state the capability the variant sacrifices.
- Assign defensible values to the parameters that most affect the result, using measurements or comparable systems where possible, and treat parameters based on subestimates as major error sources.
- Vary critical parameters, using a spreadsheet when helpful, to find what drives the model and produce conditional results rather than a single answer.
- For dependency-based project schedules, estimate each task's optimistic, most likely, and pessimistic durations instead of padding one number; use the resulting statistics as a range, not proof of accuracy, especially without comparable experience.
- Report the result with its assumptions and use units whose precision matches the evidence.
- Investigate unexpected results by checking the arithmetic, understanding, assumptions, and model.
- Deliver project work in thin slices: check requirements, prioritize risks, design and implement, integrate, and validate with users.
- Avoid fixing iteration counts or durations upfront for a nontrivial project unless the same team and technology have delivered a similar application.
- Revise the schedule after every iteration using the team's observed progress and what remains.
- Record estimates and subestimates, compare them with actual outcomes, and investigate errors greater than 50% before estimating again.
- Say “I'll get back to you” when asked for an immediate estimate, then take time to perform the analysis.
