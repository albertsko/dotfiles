## 37. Listen to Your Lizard Brain

Programming experience builds tacit pattern recognition from what works, what fails, and the likely causes of errors. Because this knowledge surfaces as reluctance, unease, fear, or disproportionate effort rather than words, treat the feeling as evidence to investigate, not proof of a specific fault. Create distance until the concern becomes concrete, lower the cost of mistakes with disposable prototypes, and look for repeated decisions before judging unfamiliar code.

### The Pragmatic Approach

- Notice signals such as avoiding a blank file, feeling uneasy about a requirement, or struggling with code that should be straightforward.
- Stop forcing progress when implementation feels unusually difficult; reassess the problem, structure, and design before adding more code and bugs.
- Step away from the keyboard and do something undemanding, such as walking or eating lunch, to give tacit knowledge time to become a concrete concern.
- Externalize the problem by sketching it or explaining it to a coworker, a nonprogrammer, or a rubber duck; listen for the point where the explanation becomes inconsistent or overly complex.
- Convert fear of mistakes into a focused experiment. Write one sentence stating what to learn, such as `Learn how the framework handles failed data binding`, then prototype only enough to answer that question.
- Stash an existing implementation when it keeps pushing back, then prototype a small analogous solution to explore the problem without fighting the production code.
- Mark prototype code as disposable, expect it to fail, and delete it even when it succeeds; carry the learning into a clean implementation instead of turning exploratory code into production code.
- Time-box an avoided or intimidating experiment to one or two hours, and commit to deleting the result when the time expires so mistakes carry no lasting cost.
- Act when vague discomfort becomes specific: challenge the assumption, simplify the design, test the edge case, or clarify the requirement that caused the concern.
- Record choices that look strange while reading unfamiliar code, look for a repeated pattern, and infer the constraints or intent behind it before changing the implementation.
- Voice unease about code, designs, and requirements in concrete terms, then investigate it with questions or small experiments instead of dismissing or blindly trusting the feeling.
- Repeat the pause, explanation, and prototype cycle when discomfort remains unresolved.
