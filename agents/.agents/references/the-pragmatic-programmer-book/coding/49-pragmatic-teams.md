## 49. Pragmatic Teams

A pragmatic team is a small, mostly stable group of about 10 to 12 people at most whose members know, trust, and depend on one another. The team multiplies individual effectiveness by owning quality collectively, detecting change early, sharing knowledge without friction, delivering end-to-end increments, automating repeatable work, and applying only enough structure to support people and produce value.

### The Pragmatic Approach

- Keep teams small and stable so members can build trust, understand one another's strengths, and communicate without excessive coordination paths.
- Make every team member responsible for quality. Fix small defects, inconsistencies, and weak tests before neglect turns them into accepted standards, and never delegate quality to a single role.
- Monitor changes to scope, deadlines, features, and operating environments. Record added requirements explicitly, for example with a burnup chart that shows both completed work and a moving target, so the team can assess changes instead of overlooking or automatically rejecting them.
- Schedule maintenance, process improvement, technology experiments, and learning alongside feature work. Work that waits for spare time will not happen.
- Evaluate new technologies with focused prototypes and analyze the results. Adopt a tool because evidence shows that it solves the team's problem, not because it is popular.
- Spread useful skills across the team through shared experiments, informal teaching sessions, or formal training. Avoid making critical knowledge depend on one person.
- Make communication immediate and low ceremony. Share progress, problems, discoveries, and current work as they emerge to prevent duplicated effort, duplicated functionality, and isolated systems.
- Present a consistent team presence outside the team. Prepare meetings, use shared terminology, and produce clear, accurate documents while preserving vigorous internal debate.
- Give the team or project a recognizable identity when it helps colleagues associate its communication and work with the team.
- Include all skills needed to take a small feature from requirement to production, such as user experience, frontend, server, data, and testing skills. Deliver thin end-to-end increments to expose delivery and communication problems quickly, then use the feedback to tune the team and its process.
- Remove gates, approvals, and handoffs that interrupt delivery. Keep implementers close enough to users to understand the context and make informed decisions.
- Automate formatting, tests, builds, and deployments so the team gets consistent, repeatable results. Develop tool-building skills inside the team instead of relying on fragile manual routines.
- Give individuals room to apply their strengths. Add only enough roles, process, and structure to support the team and deliver value, then stop.
